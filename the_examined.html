Essay · VIII    The whole point

Write the essay.

A course you only consume is entertainment. A course you answer back to is an education.

1 · Find the question, not the topic

"Stoicism" is a topic. "Is Stoic acceptance compatible with fighting injustice?" is a question — you can argue it, and you can be wrong. The essay seeds in each module are starting points, but the best question is the one that started nagging you mid-read. Write it as a single sentence with a question mark.

2 · Take a side

An essay isn't a survey of what everyone has thought. It's you committing to an answer and defending it against the strongest objection you can build against yourself. If you can't lose the argument, you're not making one.

3 · Get the sources (no university login needed)

The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy — free and genuinely rigorous. Project Gutenberg and the Internet Archive for primary texts. Google Scholar and JSTOR's free tier. And your public library card — most give free remote access to JSTOR and academic databases from home.

4 · A structure that holds

The question, and why it matters → your answer in one sentence → the argument in stages → the strongest counter-argument, taken seriously → a conclusion that's earned rather than restated. Every good essay is a version of this.

5 · Finish something

3,000–5,000 words is a real essay. But 1,500 that actually argue beats 5,000 that wander. Done and imperfect beats endless and unwritten — especially for a brain that likes a finish line.

6 · Find one honest reader

The single thing university gave that solo study lacks is someone who pushes back. Hand the finished essay to one person who'll be honest — a friend, a forum, a small publication, or me. That's where the value doubles.
